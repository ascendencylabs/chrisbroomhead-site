Why bloodwork comes first

A protocol written without data is a guess.

Most people start with the compound. The right starting point is your markers. They show where you are, what needs attention first, and how to measure whether anything you change is working.

This brief does three things:

  • Lists the markers worth asking for, grouped by system, with what each one measures.
  • Explains how to prepare so your results are reliable and comparable over time.
  • Gives you the history checklist to complete before a consultation.

It does not tell you what your results mean for you, or what to take. That depends on the full picture. That is what a consultation is for.

Where to get tested

Ask your GP first. Some of these markers are routinely available on the NHS when clinically indicated. Anything not covered can be ordered through a private UK blood testing service, usually as a finger-prick kit or a venous draw at a clinic. Venous draws are more reliable for hormones and lipids.

One rule: retest with the same lab, at the same time of day, under the same conditions. Otherwise you are comparing two different measurements, not two points in time.

The marker panel

What to ask for, and what each marker measures.

Metabolic health

Marker What it measures
Fasting glucose Blood sugar after an overnight fast.
Fasting insulin How much insulin it takes to hold that glucose level. An early window on insulin sensitivity.
HbA1c Average blood glucose over roughly the previous two to three months.

Cardiovascular

Lipid panel Total cholesterol, LDL, HDL and triglycerides.
ApoB The number of cholesterol-carrying particles that can enter artery walls.
hs-CRP High-sensitivity C-reactive protein. A marker of low-grade systemic inflammation.

Hormones

Total and free testosterone Total circulating testosterone, and the unbound fraction available to tissue.
SHBG Sex hormone binding globulin. Binds testosterone and oestradiol, so it changes how much is free.
Oestradiol The main oestrogen. Relevant for men and women.
LH and FSH Pituitary signals that drive your own hormone production.
Prolactin A pituitary hormone that affects libido, mood and other hormones.

Women: record the day of your cycle. Oestradiol and progesterone results depend on timing.

The marker panel, continued

 

Thyroid

Marker What it measures
TSH Thyroid stimulating hormone. The pituitary's signal to the thyroid.
Free T4 and free T3 The thyroid hormones themselves. T3 is the active form.

Liver and kidney

ALT, AST, GGT Liver enzymes. Raised levels can reflect liver stress. Hard training also raises ALT and AST.
Creatinine and eGFR Kidney filtration. Creatinine also rises with muscle mass and creatine use.

Blood

Full blood count Haemoglobin, haematocrit, red cells, white cells and platelets.

Nutrients

Ferritin Iron stores.
Vitamin D (25-OH) Vitamin D status.
Vitamin B12 and folate Needed for red blood cell production and nerve function.

Optional, if relevant to your goal

IGF-1 Insulin-like growth factor 1. Reflects growth hormone activity.
Morning cortisol The main stress hormone, measured at its daily peak.

How to prepare for the draw

Small changes in preparation create large changes in results.

  • Morning draw. Book between 7am and 10am. Testosterone and cortisol are highest in the morning.
  • Fasted. Nothing but water for 8 to 12 hours beforehand, unless your lab says otherwise.
  • No hard training for 48 hours. Heavy sessions can raise liver enzymes and creatinine.
  • Hydrate normally. Dehydration concentrates the blood and skews several markers.
  • Tell the lab about biotin. High-dose biotin can interfere with some hormone and thyroid tests.
  • Log your timing. If you are running a protocol, note the time of your last dose relative to the draw.
  • Same conditions every time. Same lab, same time of day, same preparation.

The history checklist

Have this written down before any consultation.

  • Your primary goal, in one sentence, with a timeframe
  • Everything you are currently running: compound, dose, frequency, start date
  • Everything you have run before, and what happened
  • Prescribed medications and supplements
  • Relevant medical history and any diagnosed conditions
  • Side effects you have noticed, and when they started
  • Training, sleep and diet: a short, honest summary
  • Your bloodwork as a PDF, with the reference ranges shown
